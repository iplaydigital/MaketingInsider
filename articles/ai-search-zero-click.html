The zero-click internet: how AI answers rewrote the top of the funnel

Search is becoming an answer engine. The brands that win the next decade are optimising to be cited, not clicked.

ML
Maya Lindqvist
Strategy editor
Nov 6, 2026·9 min read
Email

For twenty years the deal between publishers and search engines was simple. You wrote something worth finding, the engine indexed it, and it sent you a click. The click was the point. It paid for the writing, it fed the analytics dashboard, it justified the next hire.

That deal is being renegotiated in real time. AI overviews, answer panels and chat assistants now resolve a growing share of queries inside the results page. The user gets their answer; nobody gets their click. We looked at anonymised organic search data from 22 B2B and consumer sites — roughly 180 million sessions over fourteen months — and the shape of the change is remarkably consistent.

The short version

  • Informational queries are losing clicks fastest; commercial queries are holding up.
  • Answer engines reward extractable, well-sourced passages — not keyword density.
  • The clicks that remain are worth more, which changes how you should be reporting them.

What actually changed

Across the sample, zero-click searches rose from 51% to 67% of informational queries. Position-one click-through rates on those queries fell 41%. Commercial and transactional queries moved far less — 28% to 36% zero-click — which is why some teams looked at their dashboards in July, saw a modest dip, and concluded nothing had happened.

Those teams were reading an average. If you split the same data by intent, the picture is sharper: the top of the funnel has become a billboard that you pay for with effort, not a distribution channel that returns traffic. Anyone whose content strategy was “rank for the question” now owns a page that answers a question nobody clicks to read.

Answer engines reward different things

A ranking algorithm asks: is this page a good result for this query? A generative answer asks: which sentence can I lift, attribute and move on from? Those are different exams. The first rewards comprehensiveness; the second rewards extractability, specificity and corroboration.

In practice, pages that get cited tend to share four traits. They answer the question in the first forty words, then go deeper. They use the entity’s full, unambiguous name instead of “we” and “our solution”. They state facts with numbers and dates rather than adjectives. And they’re corroborated elsewhere — the same claim appearing in a directory, a review site, a trade publication or a knowledge base.

That last point is the one most teams underestimate. Language models are consensus machines. Being right is necessary but not sufficient; being consistently right across the places the model reads is what gets you cited.

Ranking was a competition for position. Being cited is a competition for consensus. Only one of those is won by the content team alone.

— the pattern across the 22 sites we reviewed

Three moves that still work

1. Write for extraction, then for depth. Restructure your highest-value pages so the answer appears before the context. Not clickbait, not a teaser — the actual answer, in a sentence, followed by the qualification. Pages written as pyramids get quoted; pages written as mysteries get skipped.

2. Build entity coverage. Decide what the model should know about you and make it true everywhere: the category you’re in, the customer you serve, the problem you solve, the proof you can point to. Then fix the inconsistencies — the stale Crunchbase profile, the missing comparison page, the product description that still uses last year’s positioning.

3. Buy the impression you can no longer earn. If the top of the funnel no longer delivers cheap attention, you either pay for it or rent it from people who own it. The brands handling this well have shifted budget from “capture demand” to “create it” — paid social, creator partnerships, category-level education — and they’re measuring branded search lift rather than session volume.

The measurement problem

Nothing in your current dashboard is built for this. Last-click attribution will show organic declining and conclude that organic is broken. It isn’t; it’s just doing a different job, one that most analytics stacks were never designed to see.

Three proxies are worth wiring up this quarter: branded search volume by market (the cleanest signal that invisible marketing is working), direct traffic to pages you never promote, and pipeline influenced by accounts that saw an unpaid impression. None is precise. All three move earlier than revenue, which is the only thing that matters for a weekly decision.

What to do this quarter

  • Audit your fifty highest-value pages for extractability: is the answer in the first paragraph, is the entity named, are the facts dated?
  • Pick twenty prompts your buyers actually type and track whether you appear, monthly, by hand. A spreadsheet beats a vendor contract at this stage.
  • Move one line item from “capture” to “create” and give it a branded-search-based success metric before it starts.
  • Change the internal reporting unit from sessions to qualified pipeline. This is the hardest one, and the only one that changes behaviour.

The uncomfortable conclusion: the zero-click internet doesn’t punish good marketing. It punishes marketing that was only ever good at being found. The work that builds memory — distinctive assets, clear positioning, consistent facts, actual reach — is suddenly the work with the best measurement story. That’s a strange gift, and it won’t last.
