First-party data is a product, not a project

The companies getting value from their data aren’t running bigger campaigns. They’re shipping better data.

DO
Daniel Osei
Data & measurement editor
Nov 3, 2026·8 min read
Email

Every marketing organisation we’ve audited in the last two years has the same asset and the same problem. The asset: a customer data platform, a warehouse, a consent management stack, several million rows of first-party events. The problem: nobody can tell you what the data is for.

Ask who consumes the “high-intent visitor” segment and you get three answers and a meeting. Ask how it’s defined and you get a SQL file last updated by someone who left. Ask how fresh it is and you get a shrug. This is not a data problem. It’s a product problem — specifically, the absence of one.

The short version

  • Collection without a consumer produces cost, not value.
  • A data product has an owner, a schema, an SLA and a changelog — like any other product.
  • Ship one segment for one decision before touching your architecture.

The collection trap

First-party data programmes usually start with a plumbing question — what should we capture? — because plumbing is tractable and procurement is easy to justify. Two years later the warehouse is beautiful and the marketing is identical. The team built a reservoir with no pipes.

The cost is not just the platform bill. It’s the opportunity cost of every decision made without the data you already own: the discount sent to people who would have bought anyway, the churn that showed up in the events three weeks before it showed up in revenue, the sales rep calling an account that’s already in an active trial.

The tell is a familiar sentence: “we’ll figure out the use cases once the data’s clean.” Data doesn’t get clean in the abstract. It gets clean against a consumer with a deadline.

What “product” actually means

Treat a data asset like software and the questions become boring in the best way. Who is the consumer? What decision does this change? What is the definition, precisely, in language a marketer and an engineer both accept? How fresh must it be? Who is on the hook when it breaks? What changed in version 4?

Answer those and you have something a lifecycle manager can build a campaign on, a sales team can trust, and a finance partner can question. Skip them and you have a dashboard nobody opens.

The most valuable data product most companies could ship this quarter is a single, well-documented definition of “active customer” that four teams agree on.

— from our audit notes

The five questions

Who consumes it? Name a person, not a department. “The lifecycle team” is not a consumer; the person who builds the win-back flow is.

What decision does it drive? If the answer is “targeting”, keep going. Which message, to whom, instead of what? A segment that changes no decision is a hobby.

What is the definition? Written down, versioned, and expressed so that a reasonable person could disagree with it. “High intent = three sessions in seven days including a pricing view” is a definition. “High intent” is a mood.

How fresh? Real-time is often a lie people pay for. Weekly is often enough for lifecycle, hourly for onsite personalisation, and never for a segment that only ever feeds a monthly report.

Who owns it when it breaks? A named owner with time allocated. Data products fail quietly; without an owner they fail for months.

Ship v1 in six weeks

Pick one decision that’s currently made on instinct and one segment that would change it. Write the definition with the person who’ll use it. Build it the ugliest way that works — a scheduled query, a CSV, a webhook. Instrument adoption, not rows: how many campaigns used it, how many decisions changed, what happened to the outcome.

Then do the unglamorous part: document it, version it, and tell everyone it exists. The failure mode of first-party data isn’t bad modelling. It’s a good segment nobody knows about.

The compounding part

The teams that get this right end up with something that looks less like a marketing asset and more like an internal product line: a small catalogue of segments, each with a consumer, a contract and a track record. Onboarding a new tool takes days instead of quarters because the definitions already exist. Privacy reviews get faster because the data flows are documented.

That’s the actual competitive advantage. Not more data — a shared, trustworthy language for describing customers, owned by someone whose job is to keep it true.
