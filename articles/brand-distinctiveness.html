Distinctiveness beats differentiation (and your brand tracking knows it)

Everyone has a positioning statement. Almost nobody has a memory structure. Here’s how to build the second one.

PR
Priya Raman
Brand editor
Oct 30, 2026·10 min read
Email

Open any brand strategy deck from the last decade and you’ll find the same slide: a two-by-two with your brand in the top-right corner, competitors scattered below, and a word like “premium” or “human” floating somewhere near the axis. It’s a differentiation exercise. It is also, almost entirely, irrelevant to whether anyone remembers you in a supermarket aisle.

Differentiation is a claim about how you’re meaningfully different from a set of alternatives. Distinctiveness is a claim about whether people can pick you out of a lineup at all. The first is a strategy document. The second is a memory structure, and it’s what actually shows up in brand tracking when the numbers move.

The short version

  • Most “differentiation” work produces a sentence nobody can recall.
  • Distinctive assets — colour, shape, character, sound, phrasing — do the recall work.
  • Audit them annually; change them roughly never.

Differentiation is a spreadsheet

The reason differentiation fails is structural. It’s produced in workshops by people looking at competitor websites, using attributes that are easy to write down and impossible to feel. The output is legible to the board and invisible to the customer, who is not comparing your positioning statement to a competitor’s — they’re recognising a colour on a shelf while pushing a trolley.

None of which means positioning is worthless. It means positioning without distinctive assets is a promise with no delivery mechanism. You can be meaningfully different and completely forgettable, and most brands are.

Distinctiveness is memory

Distinctive assets are the sensory shortcuts that let a brain identify you without effort: a colour, a shape, a character, a jingle, a layout, a recurring phrase, a face. They work because they’re repeated, consistent and slightly unusual. Repetition builds the association; consistency protects it; unusualness makes it stick.

The mistake teams make is treating these as creative decisions instead of strategic ones. A palette change is not a design preference — it’s a decision to throw away years of accumulated recognition and start again. Sometimes that’s justified. Usually it’s a new CMO wanting to leave fingerprints.

If your distinctive assets would survive a rebrand, they weren’t distinctive assets. They were decoration.

— from our audit notes

The asset audit

You can run a useful version of this in a fortnight without a research budget. Take your five most-used channels and ask three questions of each asset: is it present, is it consistent, and could a non-customer tell it was us?

  • Colour. Can you name your primary hex without looking? Does it appear on at least 60% of brand impressions across channels?
  • Shape and layout. Is there a composition template someone would recognise with the logo removed?
  • Character or face. Do you have one, and does it appear often enough to be learned?
  • Sound and motion. Do you own a sonic signature or a transition? Most brands don’t, which is an open goal.
  • Language. Is there a phrase you repeat until it belongs to you?

Then score each asset on presence, consistency and attribution. The gap between the first two scores is usually the whole story: the asset exists, it’s just used differently by every agency, region and freelancer.

Category entry points

Distinctiveness gets you recognised. Category entry points get you retrieved. People don’t buy from a consideration set built in the moment; they buy from the brands that surface when a need appears. “I need to send money abroad”, “I need this by Friday”, “I need someone to fix the boiler”.

Map the situations your category is actually bought in, then check whether your assets and messaging link to them. A brand with great recall and no retrieval cues is famous and useless. A brand with retrieval cues and no distinctiveness is a generic answer that gets compared on price.

What to stop doing

Stop redesigning the identity every time leadership changes. Stop testing individual assets in isolation and concluding that a new palette “performs better” on a two-week test that measures nothing about memory. Stop letting each campaign rebuild the visual language from scratch because the brief said “fresh”.

And stop commissioning differentiation work that never touches the production system. If the strategy doesn’t change what colour the ad is, it isn’t a strategy — it’s a paragraph.

The brands that compound are boring about this. They pick the assets, they write down the rules, they enforce them for a decade, and they let the memory do the selling. It looks like a lack of creativity from the inside. From the outside, it looks like being the brand everyone knows.
