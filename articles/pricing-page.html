The pricing page is your most underrated ad

It gets more qualified traffic than your homepage. Most teams last redesigned it three years ago.

ML
Maya Lindqvist
Strategy editor
Oct 22, 2026·6 min read
Email

There is a page on your site that receives more qualified, higher-intent traffic than your homepage, your blog and your best-performing ad combined. It’s the pricing page. And in most companies we audit, it was last meaningfully redesigned before the current product existed.

People arrive there with a budget, a problem and a shortlist. They’re not browsing; they’re deciding. Yet pricing pages are typically owned by nobody, tested rarely, and written by committee — three conditions that produce the same outcome every time: a wall of tiers with no narrative.

The short version

  • Pricing-page visitors convert at 3–5× the site average — and get a fraction of the attention.
  • Most tests worth running are about framing and objection handling, not price.
  • Hiding pricing doesn’t create mystery; it creates a bounce.

The most qualified page you own

In the B2B SaaS businesses we’ve reviewed, visitors who reach pricing convert to trial or demo at three to five times the site average. They also bounce at a higher rate than any other page, because the page answers a question they came with and either resolves it or doesn’t.

That asymmetry — high intent, high stakes, low investment — is the cheapest conversion win available to most teams. It just doesn’t feel like marketing, so it doesn’t get marketing attention.

Five tests worth running

1. Three plans, one recommendation. Two plans force a binary; four create paralysis. Three with a clearly marked “most popular” gives the brain an anchor and a default. Test the label, not just the tier.

2. Annual framing. A toggle that shows “save 20%” is weaker than showing the monthly equivalent and the annual total side by side. Test which framing people actually understand, not which looks tidier.

3. Objection-first FAQs. Write the FAQ from sales-call transcripts, not from the product roadmap. The top three objections on a pricing page are almost always integration, migration and contract terms — in that order.

4. One action per plan. “Start free”, “Talk to sales”, “Buy now” in the same card splits attention. Match the CTA to the plan’s motion and remove the alternatives.

5. Proof placement. Move the customer logo or quote from the footer of the page to directly beneath the price. Trust is needed at the moment of decision, not after it.

Most pricing-page tests don’t change the price. They change whether a stranger believes the number is for them.

— from our audit notes

What not to do

Don’t hide pricing behind a form unless your contract genuinely requires a conversation. Buyers interpret hidden pricing as “expensive and inflexible”, then arrive at the demo pre-annoyed. If you must gate it, publish a starting price and be specific about what changes it.

Don’t build decoy tiers that exist only to make the middle plan look reasonable. Sophisticated buyers notice, and the resentment is durable. Don’t ship seven tiers because four regions each added one. And don’t let a pricing page ship without a mobile pass — a third of that traffic is on a phone, where most comparison tables collapse into a scroll.

How to measure it

Track four things: pricing → trial conversion, plan mix, average revenue per new account, and sales-cycle length for leads that started on pricing. The last one is the tell. If pricing visitors close faster, the page is doing qualification work your sales team used to do.

Then put the page on a review cadence — quarterly, not annual — and give it an owner. Not a committee. A person who reads the transcripts and ships the test.
