Retention is a marketing channel now

Acquisition costs are up 34% in two years. The cheapest growth left is the customer you already paid for.

TF
Tomás Ferreira
Growth editor
Oct 27, 2026·7 min read
Email

For a decade, growth teams were paid to acquire. Cheap traffic, cheap reach, cheap attention — the arithmetic worked, so nobody looked too hard at the other end of the funnel. Then acquisition costs rose, privacy changes bit, and the same teams discovered a number that had been sitting in a spreadsheet the whole time: the customers they already had.

Retention is not a support function, a loyalty programme, or a phase that comes after growth. It’s a channel with its own economics, its own creative, and — for most subscription businesses — better unit economics than anything in the paid acquisition stack.

The short version

  • When payback periods stretch, retention is the only lever that compounds.
  • Most churn is decided in the first 30 days, not at the cancellation screen.
  • Give lifecycle a budget line and a target, or it stays a side project.

The math got worse

Across the subscription businesses we’ve looked at, blended customer acquisition cost rose roughly 34% between 2023 and 2025 while median payback stretched from about 11 months to 19. In that regime, a one-point improvement in monthly retention is worth more than a 10% cut in cost per acquisition, and it doesn’t reset when the auction gets competitive.

The reason is simple arithmetic. Acquisition buys a customer once. Retention changes how many times that customer pays, how much they refer, and how much you can afford to spend on the next one. A business with 2% monthly churn has a customer lifetime of about 50 months; at 1%, it’s 100. Nothing in the paid media stack doubles a customer’s value.

Where retention actually leaks

Almost nobody churns because the cancellation flow was too easy. They churn because the product didn’t deliver the promise the campaign made, because they never reached the moment where it became useful, or because the price stopped matching the value at renewal.

Three leaks account for most of it. First, expectation mismatch: marketing promised a workflow the product doesn’t support in week one. Second, an unowned onboarding: nobody is measured on whether a new customer reaches the activation milestone. Third, discount-led acquisition: a cohort bought at 40% off renews at full price into a value story that was never built.

Churn is usually a marketing problem that shows up in a billing system.

— from our audit notes

The first 30 days

Define the activation moment — the specific action that predicts retention at month six — and make the entire first month about getting a customer there. Not a welcome series of five emails about your values. A sequence of nudges, in the channel they actually use, engineered around one behaviour.

Then measure it weekly, by cohort, and give someone the number. Teams that do this usually find that a third of new customers never reach activation, and that the fix is product and onboarding, not more email. That’s an uncomfortable finding and a very cheap one.

The second purchase

For transactional businesses, retention is the second order. For subscriptions, it’s the first renewal and the upgrade to annual. Both are decided by whether the customer can articulate why they came back — and most can’t, because nobody ever asked them to.

The work is unglamorous: post-purchase education, replenishment timing, a reason to consolidate spend, a renewal conversation that starts 90 days out instead of 3. It doesn’t demo well in a growth review. It does show up in payback.

Own the boring channel

Email, SMS, in-app, push, direct mail — the channels with no auction and no new logo. They’re treated as legacy because they don’t come with a platform sales rep, which is exactly why they’re under-optimised and effective.

Give lifecycle a budget line, a target tied to net revenue retention, and a place in the growth meeting next to paid. Then resist the urge to measure it on open rates. The only question that matters is whether the customers you kept are worth more than the ones you’d have had to buy.
