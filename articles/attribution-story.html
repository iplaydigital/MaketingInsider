Attribution is a story we tell ourselves

Every model is a hypothesis about causality. The useful question is not which one is right — it’s which one is honest.

DO
Daniel Osei
Data & measurement editor
Oct 19, 2026·8 min read
Email

Every marketing organisation has a number that everyone quotes and nobody trusts. It’s usually in the top-left of a dashboard, it sums to 100%, and it changes depending on which platform you open. Attribution is the craft of assigning credit for a sale to the touchpoints that preceded it. It is also, unavoidably, the craft of telling a story that makes your team look effective.

None of that makes attribution useless. It makes it a tool with a very specific job — and the teams that get value from it are the ones that are explicit about what the tool can and cannot tell them.

The short version

  • All models are wrong; the useful ones are explicit about how.
  • Ask three questions before trusting any number: decision, error bar, falsifiability.
  • Report ranges and triangulate; a single point estimate is a negotiation tactic.

All models are wrong

Multi-touch attribution assumes you can observe the path. You can’t — much of it happens in dark social, on other devices, in a spreadsheet, in a Slack thread. Media mix modelling assumes stable relationships between channels and outcomes. They aren’t stable; your competitor’s budget changes and so does yours.

Platform-reported numbers assume the platform’s definition of a conversion, which is generous and self-interested. Incrementality testing assumes you can hold something out cleanly, which is true more often than vendors admit and less often than statisticians would like.

The honest position is that every method is a different lens with a different distortion. The question isn’t which lens is correct. It’s which distortion you can afford in the decision you’re about to make.

A model that changes no decision is not measurement. It’s decoration with a confidence interval.

— from our audit notes

Three honest questions

What decision does this number change? If the answer is “we report it”, you’re paying for a ritual. Attribution should sit next to a budget decision, a creative decision or a channel-mix decision. Otherwise it’s just a number with a chart.

What’s the error bar? Not the p-value on a decade-old regression — the practical range. If your model says paid social drove 18% of revenue, and you’d believe anything between 8% and 26%, say that out loud. A range invites a better conversation than a point estimate and survives being wrong.

What would falsify this? Run a geo holdout, pause a channel in one market, ask a sample of customers how they heard about you. If nothing could change your mind, you don’t have a model; you have a belief with a dashboard.

Triangulation in practice

The teams doing this well run three estimates in parallel and look for the disagreement. Platform-reported numbers give the direction and the creative signal. Media mix modelling gives the shape of the channel mix over quarters. Geo or audience holdouts give the causal check on the two or three line items big enough to be worth testing.

Where the three agree, you can act with confidence. Where they disagree, the gap is the most informative thing you own — it usually points at a measurement artefact, a tracking gap, or a channel whose reported value is mostly cannibalisation.

What to put in the board deck

One slide, three lines. What we believe is incremental, and how we tested it. What we spent to get it, by channel, with the efficiency trend. What leading indicator suggests next quarter moves in the same direction. Everything else is appendix.

If a board member asks for “the ROI of marketing” and you answer with a single number, you’ve taught them that marketing can be reduced to one. The better answer takes thirty seconds and buys you years of credibility: “Here’s the range we can defend, here’s what we’re testing next to narrow it, and here’s the decision it will change.”
